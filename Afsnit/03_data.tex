\chapter{Data}\label{sec:data}
The analysis in this paper draws on two monthly time series sourced from Eurostat, covering Denmark and the EU27 over the period from December 2000 to March 2026. The first is the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP), measured as the annual rate of change in percent \parencite{eurostat_hicp}. The second is the monthly unemployment rate, expressed as a percentage of the labour force for both sexes and all ages, not seasonally adjusted \parencite{eurostat_unemployment}. Both series are available from 1997, but the working sample is restricted to the period of overlapping observations after joining the two datasets.

\subsection{Inspections of raw data}
\textcolor{teal}{Signoff:}
\subsubsection{Looking for trend}
\ref{fig:raw_data} plots the four series over the full sample from December 2000 to March 2026. Danish and EU27 inflation follow a broadly similar trajectory: both series remain relatively stable in the range of 0 to 4 percent through most of the sample, before surging sharply in 2022 in the wake of post-COVID supply disruptions and energy price shocks, reaching approximately 10 percent for Denmark and 12 percent for the EU27. Both series have since retreated toward pre-shock levels. The co-movement between the two inflation series is visually striking and motivates the inclusion of EU27 inflation as a predictor in the multivariate framework.

The two unemployment series tell a different story. EU27 unemployment displays a clear downward trend over the sample, peaking around 11 to 12 percent during the European sovereign debt crisis in 2012 to 2013 and declining steadily thereafter. Danish unemployment shows no comparable trend, remaining in a narrower band and exhibiting a more cyclical pattern. The financial crisis of 2008 to 2009 is visible as an upward shift in Danish unemployment, followed by a gradual but incomplete recovery. The differing dynamics across the two unemployment series suggest that domestic and European labour market conditions may carry distinct information for Danish inflation.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{billeder/01_Raw_data.png}
    \caption{Both employment plots show a negative trend, and the inflations trends have a clear upward going trend}
    \label{fig:raw_data}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Initial look at the Philips curve}

The scatter plot reveals a weak negative relationship between Danish unemployment and Danish inflation over the sample period, consistent with the theoretical prediction of the Phillips curve. The fitted line slopes downward, suggesting that lower unemployment is on average 
associated with higher inflation. However, the relationship is far from tight. We see that the dispersion around the fitted line is substantial, and several observations with low unemployment are associated with very low or even negative inflation, while high inflation observations appear across the full range of unemployment rates. The post-COVID inflation surge is visible as a cluster of high inflation observations at relatively low unemployment levels in the upper left of the plot.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{billeder/02_philips_curve_denmark.png}
    \caption{Philips curve: Denmark}
    \label{fig:02_philips_curve_denmark}
\end{figure}

Overall, the scatter suggests that while a negative correlation exists between the two series, unemployment alone explains little of the variation in Danish inflation. This motivates the use of a formal forecasting framework to assess whether unemployment retains predictive value for inflation once the autocorrelation structure of inflation itself is accounted for.

\subsubsection{Danish and EU inflation co-movement}

\ref{fig:03_denmark_vs_EU_inflation} plots Danish and EU27 inflation over the full sample. The two series track each other closely throughout, displaying near-identical cyclical patterns across all major inflation episodes. Both series declined toward zero in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, briefly turned negative around 2015 amid falling energy prices and deflationary pressure in the euro area, and then surged sharply in 2022 before retreating. The EU27 series reached a slightly higher peak of approximately 12 percent compared to around 10 percent for Denmark, but the timing and shape of the shock are virtually indistinguishable.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{billeder/03_denmark_vs_EU_inflation.png}
    \caption{Danish and European inflation}
    \label{fig:03_denmark_vs_EU_inflation}
\end{figure}

The close co-movement reflects Denmark's deep integration with the European economy and its 
fixed exchange rate peg to the euro under ERM II, which ties Danish monetary conditions 
closely to those of the euro area. This suggests that EU27 inflation may carry predictive 
information for Danish inflation, motivating its inclusion as an additional predictor in the 
multivariate forecasting model.

\subsubsection{Testing for stationarity}

Before estimating the forecasting models, we assess stationarity using both the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test and the KPSS test. The ADF test has non-stationarity as the null hypothesis, while the KPSS test has stationarity as the null hypothesis.

The stationarity tests broadly confirm what the time series plots suggest. Both unemployment 
series are non-stationary in levels according to both the ADF and KPSS tests, and will be 
first-differenced prior to estimation. The inflation series, sourced directly as year-on-year 
rates of change, are already stationary transformations of the underlying price indices. 
Danish inflation is confirmed stationary by both tests, while EU27 inflation yields mixed 
results, though this is not uncommon given the sharp but transitory nature of the 2022 
inflation surge. Both inflation series are used in levels throughout the analysis.

\subsubsection{Making the data stationary}

The two unemployment series are non-stationary in levels and are therefore transformed 
using first differences:
\[
\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}.
\]
The inflation series are sourced directly as year-on-year rates of change and are used 
in levels throughout. Seasonal differencing was also considered due to indications of 
annual dependence in the seasonal plots and ACFs, but first differencing provided 
clearer evidence of stationarity. Any remaining seasonal dynamics are handled in the 
modelling stage rather than by applying seasonal differencing mechanically.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Stationarity tests in first differences}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\hline
Variable & ADF p-value & ADF conclusion & KPSS p-value & KPSS conclusion \\
\hline
Danish inflation      & $<0.01$ & Stationary & $>0.10$ & Stationary \\
EU27 inflation        & $0.022$ & Stationary & $>0.10$ & Stationary \\
Danish unemployment   & $<0.01$ & Stationary & $>0.10$ & Stationary \\
EU27 unemployment     & $<0.01$ & Stationary & $>0.10$ & Stationary \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:stationarity_differences}
\end{table}

The tests confirm stationarity across all series after first differencing, with the ADF 
tests rejecting non-stationarity and the KPSS tests failing to reject stationarity in 
every case.

\subsubsection{Structural break test (QLR)}

To test for structural instability in Danish inflation, we apply the Quandt Likelihood 
Ratio (QLR) test, which scans all candidate break dates within the central 70 percent 
of the sample and selects the date with the highest F-statistic. The sup-F statistic 
of 72.98 is highly significant ($p < 0.001$), decisively rejecting the null hypothesis 
of parameter stability.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{billeder/05_QLR_struc_break_test.png}
    \caption{Structural breaks}
    \label{fig:05_struc_break_test}
\end{figure}

The BIC-optimal partition identifies two structural breaks: December 2012 and August 
2021. The first break coincides with the tail end of the European sovereign debt crisis, 
when deflationary pressure began to suppress inflation across the euro area. The second 
break marks the onset of the post-COVID inflation surge driven by supply chain 
disruptions and energy price shocks. These breaks are consistent with the patterns 
observed in Figure~\ref{fig:ts_plot} and confirm that the inflation process has not 
been stable over the sample period. This structural instability is accounted for in 
the modelling stage through the use of indicator saturation within the GETS framework.
