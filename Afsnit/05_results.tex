The four AR terms reflect the persistence in Danish inflation, while the 
seasonal MA term confirms the residual seasonal pattern identified in the 
inspection stage. The Ljung-Box test at lag 24, however, rejects the null 
hypothesis of serially uncorrelated residuals ($Q = 38.35$, $p = 0.003$), 
indicating that the ARIMA$(4,0,1)(0,0,1)_{12}$ specification does not fully 
capture all serial dependence in the training data. This motivates the 
inclusion of external predictors in the dynamic regression models.

Figure~\ref{fig:arima_forecast} shows the 24-month out-of-sample forecast 
against actual Danish inflation. The model tracks the general level of 
inflation reasonably well over the test period, with actual values largely 
remaining within the 80 percent confidence band. However, the forecast 
intervals widen substantially over the horizon, reflecting considerable 
uncertainty about the post-surge inflation path. Out-of-sample forecast 
accuracy yields an RMSE of 0.567 and an MAE of 0.471 percentage points, 
which serve as the benchmark against which the dynamic regression models 
are evaluated.

\section{ARIMA with Structural Break Dummies}

Adding the two step dummies to the ARIMA specification yields an LM with 
ARIMA$(2,0,3)(0,0,1)_{12}$ errors. The break dummy for December 2012 carries 
a coefficient of $-0.568$ (s.e. $0.236$), consistent with the deflationary 
period following the sovereign debt crisis, while the August 2021 dummy 
carries a coefficient of $0.681$ (s.e. $0.264$), capturing the upward shift 
in the inflation mean during the post-COVID surge. The intercept of $1.991$ 
represents the baseline inflation level before either break. In-sample fit 
improves noticeably relative to the plain ARIMA benchmark, with AICc falling 
from $213.43$ to $204.04$, confirming that the structural breaks carry 
statistically meaningful information.

Out-of-sample performance tells a different story. The Ljung-Box test 
continues to reject serially uncorrelated residuals ($Q = 42.28$, $p = 0.001$), 
and forecast accuracy in the test period is marginally worse than the plain 
ARIMA, with RMSE rising from $0.567$ to $0.572$ and MAE from $0.471$ to 
$0.509$. This divergence between in-sample and out-of-sample performance is 
consistent with a well-known limitation of structural break dummies in 
forecasting: once the break has occurred and the new regime has settled, the 
dummies provide little additional signal for future values. The plain ARIMA 
benchmark therefore remains the more competitive model out-of-sample, and 
serves as the reference point for the dynamic regression evaluation.

\section{GETS Model Selection and Dynamic Regression}

The GUM included AR lags 1 to 6 of Danish inflation, contemporaneous and six 
lags of first-differenced Danish unemployment, EU27 inflation, and 
first-differenced EU27 unemployment, together with the two structural break 
dummies. After GETS reduction, no lag of Danish unemployment was retained — 
all were eliminated as statistically insignificant. EU27 inflation at lags 0, 
1, and 6 survived reduction, confirming the ERM II spillover channel identified 
visually in the data section. The 2013 break dummy was retained ($p = 0.003$), 
while the 2021 break dummy was not significant individually ($p = 0.592$), as 
the IIS and SIS indicators absorbed the post-COVID inflation shock directly. 
The residual diagnostics from the GETS model pass cleanly, with Ljung-Box 
$p = 0.776$, confirming adequate fit.

The GETS-selected specification is carried into the fpp3 framework as Model C, 
a dynamic regression of Danish inflation on EU27 inflation at lags 0, 1, and 6, 
with both structural break dummies retained for economic reasons. AICc 
minimisation selects an LM with ARIMA$(1,0,2)(1,0,2)_{12}$ errors. The 
estimated coefficients are reported in Table~\ref{tab:modelC_coef}.

In-sample fit improves substantially relative to all ARIMA specifications, with 
AICc falling to 94.78 and residual variance dropping to 0.073. The contemporaneous 
EU27 inflation coefficient of 0.696 is large and precisely estimated, consistent 
with the tight co-movement observed in the data. The Ljung-Box test, however, 
continues to indicate residual serial correlation ($Q = 34.01$, $p = 0.013$).

Out-of-sample, Model C performs worse than the univariate ARIMA benchmark, 
yielding RMSE of 0.767 and MAE of 0.637 against 0.567 and 0.471 respectively. 
The substantial gap between in-sample and out-of-sample performance suggests 
that the model has overfit to the training period. The EU27 inflation signal, 
while highly informative in-sample, appears to amplify rather than reduce 
forecast errors in the 2024 to 2026 test window, a period in which both 
Danish and EU27 inflation were rapidly normalising from post-surge levels. 
This result directly answers the research question: incorporating unemployment 
and European macroeconomic variables does not improve forecast accuracy for 
Danish inflation relative to a simple univariate benchmark.

\section{Model Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} summarises out-of-sample forecast accuracy 
across all five models over the 24-month test period.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Out-of-sample forecast accuracy (test period: April 2024 to March 2026)}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
Model & RMSE & MAE \\
\hline
ARIMA baseline             & 0.567 & 0.471 \\
ARIMA with break dummies   & 0.572 & 0.509 \\
Model A (DK unemployment)  & 0.613 & 0.544 \\
Model C (GETS: EU27 inflation) & 0.767 & 0.637 \\
Model B (DK unemployment + EU27 inflation) & 1.208 & 1.039 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:model_comparison}
\end{table}

The univariate ARIMA benchmark produces the lowest RMSE and MAE across all 
models. Every multivariate specification performs worse out-of-sample, and the 
ranking is monotone: adding more predictors consistently worsens forecast 
accuracy. Model B, which includes both Danish unemployment and EU27 inflation, 
is the worst performer by a wide margin, with RMSE more than double that of the 
benchmark. This is a stark result: not only does unemployment fail to improve 
forecasts, but combining it with EU27 inflation actively harms performance. The 
result is consistent with a well-documented finding in the inflation forecasting 
literature that multivariate models often fail to outperform univariate 
benchmarks out-of-sample, particularly over short horizons and in periods of 
regime change \parencite{stock_watson_2008}.

\section{TSCV Robustness Check}

Time Series Cross-Validation over the full sample yields an RMSE of 0.399 and 
MAE of 0.274 for the ARIMA baseline, based on one-step-ahead forecasts averaged 
across all expanding windows. These figures are lower than the fixed test set 
results, which is expected since one-step-ahead errors are inherently smaller 
than 24-step-ahead errors. The TSCV results confirm that the ARIMA model 
produces consistent and stable one-step-ahead forecasts across the full sample, 
reinforcing its position as the strongest performing model in this analysis.
